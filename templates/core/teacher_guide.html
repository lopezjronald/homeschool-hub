The Discovery Method

What each week asks of the girls, what it asks of you, and what good work looks like at their age. Written as one screen you can check mid-week — the book stays the source of record.

One book, five weeks

Four weeks of reading and responding, then a fifth week to make something. Sections 1–4 are the same five moves every week; Section 5 is the project.

Your two jobs

The method is deliberately light on you. Each week there are two things to do, and neither is marking.

1

Read alongside her

Stay a section ahead, or read it together. The point isn't to steer her toward the right answer — it's to notice things out loud so she starts noticing her own. Point at one good sentence, not twelve.

2

Talk about her writing

She reads her paragraph to you before you touch a pen. You respond to the idea first — where it jumps a step, where it goes vague — and only then to spelling. The order matters more than it sounds.

The trap worth naming: a conference that opens with a spelling correction teaches her the spelling was the point. The guide is blunt about this — never use the conference to crush the idea.

What each move is actually for

Read4 pts

Read the whole section before touching the guide — keeping the story intact is the priority. Skim the comprehension questions and the vocabulary first, so she reads on the lookout rather than reading blind and reconstructing afterwards.

What you're listening for: point of view, a word that's doing unusual work, a sentence whose rhythm you can hear. Tag them as you go — sticky notes, margins. Breadcrumbs, not a dissection.

Journal4 pts

Character, setting, plot — but the interesting half is her opinion of them. A character trait is only worth writing down with an example from the story attached: courageous is a claim, and the sentence after it is the evidence.

The lever here is vocabulary. Push past nice and good. A thesaurus on the table changes this exercise more than any instruction does.

Acquire2 pts

Words from the book, defined and then used. The word list is a sample, not a syllabus — the real goal is that she starts noticing exceptional words on her own.

The sentence is the whole exercise. "I saw a picture" is a complete sentence and worth nothing. Ask who, what, where, when, why — a dull sentence becomes a real one.

Recollect3 pts

A second, directed pass through the plot — the questions run in story order on purpose, so answering them walks her back through the section and lets her join the dots she missed the first time.

Complete sentences, always. The move to teach first is echoing the question back: Who thought up the game? → Peggy thought up the game. Once that's automatic, push for a sentence that says something the question didn't.

Explore7 pts

Two halves: she writes an original paragraph, and you talk about the book. This is where the marks are, and it's the part most worth your hour.

Where the twenty points sit

Worth knowing before you mark anything: over a third of the week rides on Explore, and reading itself is worth as much as the whole journal.

How a paragraph gets made

Five stages, and the order is load-bearing. Mechanics come last, on purpose — worrying about commas during the first draft is what kills the idea before it lands.

  1. Brainstorm

    No sentences yet. Fill a blank page with words, phrases, drawings. Get fodder.

  2. First draft

    Wrangle the idea onto the page inside a paragraph. Ignore spelling entirely.

  3. Read & confer

    She reads it aloud to you first, red pen in her own hand. Then you respond — to the idea.

  4. Revise

    Rewrite, reread. Now the mechanics.

  5. Polish

    Copy it out clean, for a reader who isn't you.

The shape of the paragraph itself: open with something that makes a stranger want the next sentence · develop it with evidence · close with a turn that leaves them thinking. Hook, body, clincher.

Marking the writing

Four points, five bands. The differences are about idea and structure far more than about errors.

Accomplished4

Creative focus, ideas that build, varied sentences, word choice you notice, conventions handled.

Proficient3

On topic with real support, a logical order, some sentence variety, adequate but unremarkable choices.

Basic2.5

The topic is in there but unclear, support is thin, sentences sit still.

Limited2

Topic mentioned rather than addressed, weak organisation, fragments and run-ons.

Poor1

No clear topic or support, structure missing, frequent basic errors.

Don't let Section 5 start in week 5

This is the single most practical thing in the whole guide, and the easiest to miss. The project is planned across the four weeks before it — so that week five is building, not deciding.

Why this matters here specifically: the hands-on projects in this app need clay, mixed paint, card, real cloth. Deciding on the Monday of week five means an afternoon lost to a shop. Ask her in week two what she's thinking of making.

Running the discussion

Three points a week, and the part that changes how she thinks. It is a conversation, not an oral exam.

Do

  • Follow the conversation where it naturally wants to go
  • Rephrase a question she doesn't get, rather than moving on
  • Add your own questions, and let her add hers
  • Let her act it out, or argue the other side, if that's what's alive

Don't

  • Accept "I dunno" — that's where the thinking starts
  • Panic when it drifts, as long as she's genuinely thinking
  • Feel obliged to get through every question
  • Fill the silence yourself

What to expect from each of them

The guide is written in Levels 1–3 — but the level belongs to the workbook, not the child. Check the book in front of you before trusting the grade.

Which level is this guide? Open its Acquire page.

Level 1

The definitions are printed, numbered, and she matches them — then fills the words into ready-made sentences.

Level 2 or 3

There are no definitions. She looks each word up herself and writes her own sentence.

Violet's Hundred Dresses guide prints the definitions, so it is a Level 1 book — regardless of what the folder it came in is called.

Careful with the word "level" elsewhere in this app. The grade dropdowns say Level 3, Level 7 and so on — those are GRADES, nothing to do with Blackbird's Levels 1–3. On this page a guide level is always written "Guide Level".
Reading stamina is the quiet measure. By the end of a Level 2 year, 10–20 minutes at a stretch with real retention. By the end of Level 3, 30–60 minutes — and thinking about it afterwards.
