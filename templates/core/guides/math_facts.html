Division is not a second table

The strongest finding in the whole research pile: people who know their facts don't hold a division table at all — they flip the question. The sentence that unlocks every division fact is a times question with a hole in it:

“Eight times what makes forty-eight?”

Say it that way round, every time. Not “forty-eight shared between eight” — sharing invites her to deal objects out one by one, which is the counting stage she needs to leave. And when she answers, have her say the division sentence back: “so 48 ÷ 8 is 6.” She reasons in multiplication but rehearses the division voice.

Two traps worth naming before they bite. Division cares about order — after a year of “6 × 8 is the same as 8 × 6” she will assume 2 ÷ 4 = 4 ÷ 2; say out loud that multiplication doesn't care and division cares a lot. And zero is two different problems: 0 ÷ 8 is 0 (nothing shared out is nothing each), but 8 ÷ 0 has no answer at all — “what times zero makes 8? Nothing does.” Same flip-it tool she already uses.

Five minutes at the kitchen table

Cover a corner 60 seconds, no materials

Draw a triangle on a napkin: 48 at the top, 6 and 8 at the bottom corners. Cover the 48 with your thumb — she says “six times eight is forty-eight”. Cover the 6 — “forty-eight divided by eight is six”. Cover the 8 — “forty-eight divided by six is eight”. Then go faster and cover corners at random. One triangle, not three facts.

Rotate the rectangle 3 minutes, cereal or Lego

Lay out 48 of anything in 6 rows of 8. “How many eights in forty-eight?” — she counts the rows: six. Now turn the whole rectangle 90°. Nothing added, nothing taken away — but now it's 8 rows of 6, and “how many sixes in forty-eight?” is eight. The turn is the whole lesson: same total, different question. It's also the cure for thinking division works both ways round.

Salute 5 minutes, a deck of cards

Aces to 10s only. You hold one card face-down as yours, show her one card, and announce the product. She works out your hidden card — and says how: “You have 8, because 6 times 8 is 48.” That sentence, spoken dozens of times, is the missing-factor strategy practising itself without a single flashcard. To sharpen the tricky ones, strip the deck to just 4s, 6s, 7s and 8s.

A worked script: 6, 8 and 48

One hard fact family, from multiplication through both divisions, the way it sounds out loud. Reuse the shape on 7 × 8 = 56.

You
Make me a rectangle — six rows, eight in each. How many altogether?
Her
Five eights is forty… one more eight… forty-eight.
You
That's exactly the move. Now — how many eights are in forty-eight?
Her
(counting rows) Six.
You
So forty-eight divided by eight is six. Now watch — I'm turning the rectangle. Did I add or take anything?
Her
No. It's still forty-eight… but now it's eight rows of six.
You
So how many sixes in forty-eight?
Her
Eight! Forty-eight divided by six is eight.
You
Four facts, one rectangle. Last one, no rectangle: forty-eight divided by six?
Her
…eight. Because six times eight is forty-eight.

When she stalls, don't give the answer — re-voice it as the times question: “six times what makes forty-eight?” Every division is a times question with a hole in it, and that's the trick she'll use forever.
